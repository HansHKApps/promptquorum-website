0. How to read this report

Every statement carries one of the labels below. A statement without a label is connective text and contains no factual claim. Where a number comes from a secondary report rather than the original publisher, the source list says so. Sources older than twelve months are marked older than 1 year in Section 11.

FACT Reported by a named third-party source (citation given).
AUTHOR-MEASURED Measured or observed by the author in PromptQuorum hands-on tests; evidence labels from the tests are kept.
CALCULATION Arithmetic performed on cited figures; the calculation is shown.
THIRD-PARTY FORECAST A third-party projection, not an observation.
ASSUMPTION An inference. The box names who makes it and what it rests on.
UNVERIFIED Stated by the author; no supporting data found yet.

Disclosure and method limits

  • The author founded PromptQuorum, which operates a content site, a directory of local-AI applications and a desktop application for multi-model prompting. Applications in the directory receive traffic from the site. No payment, sponsorship or vendor contact is recorded in the test notes.
  • The hands-on sample is nine tests by one tester, eight on one 24 GB Apple Mac (recorded as M3 Pro in earlier records; the LU record corrects its own “M3” note to M4, so the chip is recorded inconsistently) and one on an Android phone (12 GB). It is a convenience sample, not a random sample of the app market. The tester founded PromptQuorum, which lists the tested apps; for LU the developer supplied a founder statement and exchanged messages with the tester before and after the test (disclosure in the test record). No payment is recorded.
  • Several test records list the tester's own errors (for example, choosing the wrong model type, or judging features that were visible but not evaluated). These are kept in the underlying test pages and are summarised here where they affect a finding.
  • Consumer-spending data in this report is almost entirely US-only. No comparable EU household data was found.

2. Thesis and definitions

2.1 The thesis

The classic chicken-and-egg problem asks which comes first. For local AI the problem is different: both sides are scarce at the same time. Few computers can run capable local models, and few applications exist that give a non-technical user a reason to buy such a computer.

2.2 Who the report is about

The reference user is a non-technical user who expects to download an app, understand what it does, optionally add a local model through Ollama or a direct download, and use it without a terminal, Python, or Linux. Developers and enterprise IT, who are today's main local-AI users, are discussed only as a contrast.

ASSUMPTION Made by: Author (Hans Kuepper)

Enterprises and developers are the early market for local AI hardware; private end users follow later.

Based on: Author's market observation. Supporting data in this report: coverage describes Ollama as a developer platform, and DGX Spark is positioned as a developer system. No consumer-versus-enterprise split of local-AI users was found.

3. Few chickens: the hardware base

3.1 Best available data: the Steam Hardware Survey

Two different memories. A computer has system memory (RAM), which every program shares, and a graphics card has its own graphics memory (VRAM). A local AI model has to fit into one place to run at a usable speed, on a PC mostly the graphics card. This report therefore counts graphics memory (VRAM) only. Main-memory sizes are left out because they do not decide whether a PC can run a model: a PC can have 32 GB of RAM and 8 GB of VRAM. Macs and some other designs share one pool, so for them the single memory figure is the one that counts.

No public dataset reports the share of all personal computers that can run a given local model. The closest public proxy is Valve's monthly Steam survey. It is opt-in, covers gamers, and is therefore likely to overstate capability compared with the general PC population.

[S1, S2]

FACT The July 2026 survey shows 16 GB VRAM as the largest single bucket for the first time (25.90%).[S1] FACT Only 5.43% of respondents had 24 GB of VRAM, and a 32 GB bucket was not found in the retrieved reports. FACT macOS accounted for 1.92% of Steam systems in September 2026, so the survey says little about Mac users.[S2]

Key takeaway. Among opted-in gamers, the 16-32 GB range is now common, but 24 GB or more of VRAM is a minority. For the general population of laptops and PCs no comparable data exists, and the true share is probably lower (assumption above).

3.2 The system uses memory before the model does

A model size that fits in total memory does not fit in free memory. The author's tests give measured values on a 24 GB Apple Mac.

AUTHOR-MEASURED Activity Monitor showed 9.67 GB used right after startup and 12.92 GB used in a first capture with a browser, office and chat applications open.[S22] AUTHOR-MEASURED The Unsloth app refused a 17 GB image model; it counted about 2 GB usable (7 GB free) before a reboot and about 6 GB usable (11 GB free) after.[S22] CALCULATION 24 − 9.67 = 14.33 GB left after startup, which is below the 17 GB the app stated for that model.

AUTHOR-MEASURED A 27B-parameter model in 4-bit format ran in chat at 11.0 tokens per second on the same machine, but reading a web page and an event search both ended in "Ran out of memory" (at 34.4k context).[S22]

ASSUMPTION Made by: Author (Hans Kuepper)

Even 32 GB of VRAM may not be enough for a normal user who keeps an editor and other applications open next to the model.

Based on: Author's reasoning from the 24 GB measurements above. No test on a 32 GB machine exists in the data; the statement is an extrapolation.

AUTHOR-MEASURED Guides often say macOS uses about 4 GB. In the author’s normal use of a 24 GB Mac the system used 9.67 GB right after startup. The guide figure describes a bare system; the measured figure is what an ordinary user experiences, and it is the one that decides how much memory is left for a model. The figures are from a normal working machine, not a laboratory set-up.[S22]

4. Even fewer eggs: applications and willingness to pay

4.1 How many people pay for AI at all

The author's starting point was that about 2-3% of US households pay for AI, so a "free" local alternative has limited appeal. Three independent US card datasets confirm a low single-digit share. This report uses only that range.

[S11, S12, S13]

FACT Card data from PNC (2.2%, May 2026), Bank of America (~3%) and YipitData via a16z (4.5%, up from 2.1% a year earlier) agree on a low single-digit share.[S11, S12, S13] FACT The top 1% of payers account for 19.5% of observed consumer AI spend, averaging $903 per month.[S13]

4.5 Supply of applications: the vibe-coding argument

ASSUMPTION Made by: Author (Hans Kuepper)

Within one to two years, hundreds of thousands of apps will exist, including local-AI apps, because vibe coding lowers the cost of building software.

Based on: Author's direct experience building PromptQuorum applications with AI coding tools, and press reports about software companies being replaced by in-house builds. No time series of local-AI app counts was found to test the forecast.

FACT A figure of $285 billion in software-stock value lost in 48 hours in early February 2026 is attributed to vibe-coding fears in one derivative article; the original could not be verified.[S25] It is not used as evidence in this report.

FACT Hugging Face reports that 1.5% of repositories account for 99.2% of downloads and that 85.6% of models have fewer than 200 lifetime downloads.[S19] ASSUMPTION Inference from that distribution: a large number of new apps does not by itself imply a large number of used apps; usage in open ecosystems is highly concentrated.

Key takeaway. The application layer is real but small and fragmented. Funding exists for runtimes, not clearly for consumer applications. Claims about the number of apps need a counted series, which the quarterly format can provide.

5. Local models versus frontier models

A frequent claim is that local models are six to twelve months behind the frontier, or only about four months for the largest. The author regards this as a marketing argument: it is offered as a reason to switch, and it does not describe what an ordinary machine can run. The source for the figures, Epoch AI, defines them for specific model classes.

[S14, S15, S16]
  • FACT Since January 2026 the most capable open-weight models lag the closed frontier by about four months; the earlier figure was three months.[S14, S16]
  • FACT Models small enough for one consumer GPU reach frontier-level benchmark scores with a lag of 6-12 months.[S15]
  • FACT Hugging Face reports that Chinese labs released the largest open model almost every month of 2026, with ceilings between 754 billion and 2.78 trillion parameters.[S19]
  • CALCULATION A model of 754 billion parameters stored in 4-bit format needs more than 350 GB for weights alone (754 x 0.5 bytes), well above any consumer memory in Section 3. The 4-month figure therefore describes models few users can run.

In plain terms. Two different lags are quoted and they describe different things. "About 4 months" compares the best open model with the best closed model; the best open models are too large for any consumer machine. "6-12 months" compares models small enough for one consumer GPU with 24-32 GB of memory with the frontier. Neither figure covers a computer with less memory.

FACT Epoch adds that the best-scoring open model on a benchmark is more likely to be over-fitted to it, so the lag in everyday usefulness is probably longer than the benchmark lag.[S15]

ASSUMPTION Made by: Author (Hans Kuepper)

The "local models are only months behind" statement is a marketing argument and cannot be used in practice by the reference user.

Based on: Table above: the 4-month figure covers models needing about 377 GB or more; the 6-12 month figure covers models that need a 24-32 GB GPU and is older than 1 year; no figure exists for a typical laptop or phone. Tests (S22): a 27B model on a 24 GB Mac ran out of memory on web-page reading and an event search; Caelum, a 1 GB model on a phone, gave a false news answer and a logic answer rated 5/25 by ChatGPT as judge (judge not independent, not verified). Limits: few tests, one tester, no side-by-side local-versus-cloud benchmark on the same tasks.

Key takeaway. The lag figures are real on benchmarks, but they describe models users cannot run (4 months) or older consumer-GPU data (6-12 months, older than 1 year). In the author’s view they are a marketing argument, because for a typical laptop or phone no figure exists and the tests show failures.
